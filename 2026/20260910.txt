A Jungian "complex" is not the pop-psychology version, an unresolved issue you can name and move past. [Stephen Johnson lays out the actual clinical picture](https://bigthinkmedia.substack.com/p/mini-personalities-why-carl-jung): a complex is a cluster of feelings, memories and thoughts organized around one emotional theme, usually rooted in an experience the conscious mind never fully digested. You recognize one firing by its signature, drawing on six markers the Jungian scholar John Haule listed in 1984: it produces real bodily effects, a tightening chest, a racing pulse; it acts with a will that isn't quite yours; it behaves less like a symptom and more like another self; it dramatizes itself in dreams and art; it can surface as a hallucinated voice; and in its most extreme form it can take over the psyche entirely.

The radical part of Jung's claim isn't that these clusters exist. It's that he meant the autonomy literally: complexes, he wrote, can resist conscious intention and come and go as they please. The therapist Richard Schwartz built an entire method, Internal Family Systems, out of watching this happen in a patient with bulimia who described internal voices distinct enough, and in conflict enough, that he could interview them separately. You don't just have complexes. Under the right conditions, they answer back.

[The rhizome](post.html?p=2026/20260906.txt) argued something adjacent from the outside: Deleuze's objection to archetype theory was that a single center with branches growing out of it is a habit of thought, not a fact about minds. Complex theory, even coming from inside the tradition Deleuze was criticizing, already half-agrees. If part of your psyche can act on its own schedule and resist the part that calls itself "you," the mind was never really run from one address.

!!A part of you that resists your conscious intentions isn't a metaphor. Jung meant it as a clinical description.!!
