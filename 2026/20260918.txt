↳ [back to index](index.html)

Carl von Clausewitz, the Prussian officer who wrote *On War* after watching Napoleon rewrite the rules of it, coined "friction": the accumulation of small resistances, tired men, bad weather, wrong information, that turns an obvious move into an exhausting one. His line for it: everything in war is simple, and the simplest thing is difficult. The complexity people imagine into strategy is mostly an excuse — the moves are usually obvious, executing them under friction is what's hard.

His other line follows from the first: the enemy of a good plan is the dream of a perfect one. [A plan doesn't have to be perfect, it just has to exist](post.html?p=2026/20260107.txt) — waiting for the flawless version is how the good-enough version never ships.

Hemingway put the same friction inside a person instead of an army. In *A Farewell to Arms*: the world breaks everyone, and afterward many are strong at the broken places. Not despite the break. At it.

!!The break isn't what stops you. Waiting for a plan that can't break is.!!
