"This is toxic" and "I feel that this is toxic" describe the same experience. Only one of them can be argued with.

[Joshua Fields Millburn's suggestion](https://www.theminimalists.com/p554/) is to stop issuing feelings as verdicts. The categorical version invites a counter-verdict, and the conversation becomes a contest over who read the situation correctly. The prefixed version invites a question instead: why does it land that way for you and not for me.

Feelings aren't wisdom. Treating them as evidence about how things are is how most sterile arguments start. But they are information, usually the only available window into what someone fears or wants.

[Most moral disagreements turn out to be disagreements about facts](post.html?p=2026/20260327.txt). The ones that survive the facts are the ones where the feeling was the real content all along, which is exactly when naming it as a feeling stops being a concession and becomes accurate.

!!Stating a feeling as a fact doesn't make it stronger. It only makes it contestable.!!
