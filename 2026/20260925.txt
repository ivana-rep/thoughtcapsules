Lewis Mumford's claim about the machine, as [Jared Henderson reads it](https://open.substack.com/pub/jaredhenderson/p/what-comes-after-the-machine), is that technology never advanced for the reasons it gave. Efficiency was the justification offered afterward. The drives underneath were power, order, and something closer to holiness: the machine as an object of faith rather than a tool with a use case.

Mumford also wrote the alternative, an economy organized around cultural creation, with art, science and tradition as the real sites of value and mechanical progress demoted to infrastructure. None of it arrived. The leisure the machine was supposed to hand back never showed up for most people, and the prescription now reads as the weakest part of the work.

That gap deserves attention on its own terms. The diagnosis has held for ninety years. The solution didn't survive the decade it was written in, and the man who saw the mechanism most clearly was no better than anyone else at predicting what would come of it.

[Faith in the machine did the coordinating work a shared fiction does](post.html?p=2026/20260731.txt), which is why noticing it was never enough to dissolve it.

!!Seeing why something is broken and knowing what should replace it are separate abilities. Nothing guarantees the same person has both.!!
