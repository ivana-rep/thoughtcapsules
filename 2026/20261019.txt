Why aren't smart people happier? An essay [collected by Thomas Oppong](https://postanly.substack.com/p/why-smart-people-are-not-happier), who curates a weekly newsletter on psychology and philosophy, gives a simple answer. Intelligence, as we measure it, is the skill of solving well-defined problems: clear terms, one right answer, like a math test. Most of life isn't like that. How to live well, how to repair a friendship, what to do with today: nobody hands you the terms, and there's no answer key. The skill those problems need has no good name. "Wisdom comes the closest," the author writes. Being smart doesn't make you good at it.

The diagnosis is accurate, and it leaves a question open. If life's problems come without terms, something has to set them: what counts as a good answer? Further down the same collection, Oppong offers one through the historian Will Durant, who borrows a line from the philosopher Spinoza: "desire is the very essence of man." We are what we want, so what we want sets the terms.

That's where it falls short. If you are your desires, wisdom can only be the skill of serving them well, and the problem stays poorly defined, because nothing tells you which desire should win. Scripture starts from the other end: [the fear of the Lord is the foundation of wisdom](https://ivana-rep.github.io/soul/post.html?p=bible/proverbs_9-10.txt). Fear here means reverence: accepting that you are not the measure of what is good. And desire isn't a fixed essence. [Wherever your treasure is, there the desires of your heart will also be](https://ivana-rep.github.io/soul/post.html?p=bible/matthew_6-19-34.txt#v21). Change what you treasure and the desires follow. [Reason cut off from the question of good](post.html?p=2026/20260302.txt) is only cleverness.

!!Wisdom isn't serving your desires well. It's knowing which ones to serve.!!
