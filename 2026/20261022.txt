"Beware the barrenness of a busy life." Barren is the word for a field that yields no harvest: a life can be full of work and still produce nothing that lasts. The line is usually attributed to Socrates, though no ancient text contains it.

[Thomas Oppong](https://postanly.substack.com/p/socrates-on-the-barrenness-of-a-busy), who writes a weekly newsletter on philosophy and self-improvement, reads it as a warning: the problem isn't being active, it's mistaking activity for meaning. He suggests two remedies. Track for one week where your hours actually go. Keep fifteen quiet minutes a day to think. From Marcus Aurelius, the Roman emperor and Stoic philosopher, he borrows a filter: "Ask yourself at every moment, 'is this necessary?'"

The remedies are sound. [Time lost looks like activity](post.html?p=2026/20260511.txt), and tracking it makes it visible.

The diagnosis stops early. Tracking shows where the hours go, not why you keep filling them. Busyness is rarely a planning error. It keeps silence away, and it proves, hour after hour, that you are needed. When being busy is what makes you feel you count, the answer to "is this necessary?" is always yes. The Gospels show another way. Crowds pressing in, every need real, and still [Jesus often withdrew to the wilderness to pray](https://ivana-rep.github.io/soul/post.html?p=bible/luke_5-16.txt). Not to get organized, but to be with the Father, whose love never depended on how much He did. Fifteen quiet minutes spent reviewing yourself change little. Spent in front of God, they change a lot.

!!Busyness is rarely a scheduling problem. It's usually a way of proving you count.!!
